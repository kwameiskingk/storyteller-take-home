The situation

What the service is, where the pilot stands, and who has asked for what.

Storyteller is piloting a content opportunity service for the Aurora Basketball League's digital editors. Approved feeds from the league, the teams and a broadcast partner are polled every five minutes. An AI step decides whether each item is relevant; a second step writes a recommendation card with a headline, a summary, a priority and source links. An operator, Sam, approves cards before the client sees them. Two client editors then decide what to publish on the league's app and website. The service does not publish anything itself.

The Finals are tied 1-1. Game 3 is on Friday 11 September at 19:00 UTC, eight days from now. The client has planned editor training for Wednesday 9 September. This morning, Jordan Reed, the client's Director of Digital Content, reported that a Game 2 result card appeared more than once in a sample they were sent, and that a card claimed Tayo Mensah had set a league record when he had tied a Waves record. Jordan asked whether they can still count on the service for the Finals, and whether CourtsideWire, an external outlet that was ahead of the official team account on an injury story, could be added.

Why not full scope

In the ten-item replay the pilot produced a duplicate card from a retry bug, fabricated a record claim at 0.94 confidence, lost the player availability update without telling anyone, rejected the tipoff change as routine, and ranked a sponsor competition as high-priority player news. That is five faults in nine approved items. At forecast volume, 240 cards in six hours with peaks of 80 an hour, the same rates would put wrong or missing information in front of the editors during the Finals. The operator reviews 30 to 40 cards an hour and leaves thirty minutes before tipoff. Full scope fails on evidence and on arithmetic.

Why not withdraw

The causes are specific and each has an owner. The duplicate and the lost item are bounded engineering fixes that Marta can size for Friday. The record claim, the rejected schedule change and the promotion are instruction and process changes, already drafted and tested outside production. The client keeps its safety net. Withdrawing from a fixable problem gives up the one event Product wants to prove the service on, and tells the client we could not recover from two faults in a week.

CourtsideWire

The client is right that CourtsideWire was ahead: 22 minutes before the Waves' official medical update. The report also rested on "two people described as being close to the team" and said Cole was "expected to miss Game 3", where the team said "questionable, game-time decision". One item cannot show whether a source is reliable, and these two claims differ. Adding the source needs an ingestion change from Marta, a change to what Priya has told the client, and a product decision from Lewis on how an unconfirmed report should look. None of that fits in eight days alongside the fixes above.

After Game 3 I would run CourtsideWire in the background for four weeks, invisible to the client, and record two things for every story: how many minutes ahead of an official source it was, and whether an official source later confirmed it, contradicted it, or never addressed it. If the lead is real and contradictions are rare, I would propose a labelled unconfirmed-report lane, designed with Lewis. Those numbers, not opinions, decide it.

Volume comes down in three layers before it reaches a person. Marta's fix removes duplicates at source: in the trial, one item in nine produced two cards. The revised relevance instruction rejects sponsor promotions and marks them so the approver can see they were seen: another one in nine. The two-lane split then keeps only results, availability, schedule and venue changes and official records live during the game. Four of the nine approved sample items are live-lane types. Applied to the forecast peak, about 36 cards an hour reach the approver.

That is the limit of one person at Sam's observed rate, with no slack for a hard case. My own model of review time, built from the pack's 45 to 60 seconds for a quick check and two to three minutes for a hard one, gave about 50 cards an hour. Sam's measured rate is lower, so the measured rate is what the plan uses. Three consequences follow.

  1. A second approver from 18:30 UTC is an explicit ask of Lewis and Priya, not an assumption. Sam's agreed cover ends at 18:30; tipoff is 19:00. Without a second approver, from tipoff through the result and the post-game peak the feed depends on one person who is also reconciling exports and handling escalations.
  2. Queue dwell is a stop condition, not a hope.
  3. Monday's logged session replaces the 44 percent sample figure with a measured one before the client is told anything.

The ten-minute target

The client wants developments surfaced within about ten minutes of appearing in an approved source. The feed polls every five minutes, so up to five minutes are gone before anything happens. Generation took 7 to 21 seconds in the replay, about 36 seconds with one timeout and retry. A straightforward review is about a minute. That leaves roughly three and a half minutes for a card to wait in the queue. Achievable for one approver who does nothing else. Not achievable if that person is also reconciling and escalating through the result peak.

On the night, each with how it is observed

There is no live telemetry and no engineer on duty, so a stop condition nobody can see is decoration. Each of these names its observer.

  • A factual error reaches the client. Observed by client report or by the approver.
  • Cards wait more than ten minutes for 30 consecutive minutes. Observed from decision log timestamps against card generation times.
  • No approver online.
  • Two duplicate cards from one source in the queue, or retries per call above 0.25 over 30 minutes if Marta's 15-minute export ships. This is Finance's trigger, because spend itself cannot be seen live.

Any one of these pauses the feed. Priya sends a pre-written message and the editors continue with manual monitoring. The message is in the runbook.

What would change the recommendation

  • To full scope: a clean 20-card set, Monday's session showing the slow-review share well under the assumed 20 percent, and Lewis releasing the held lane.
  • To withdraw: Marta ships item 1 only. The client will not keep manual monitoring. Monday's session shows Sam under 30 cards an hour with the checklist. Or no second approver and a measured live-lane share well above 44 percent.

What the findings mean

The duplicate is a plumbing fault, not a writing fault. The request for the Game 2 recap was declared timed out at 15 seconds and a retry started one second later. Six seconds after that the original response arrived and was written to the queue. Six seconds after that the retry arrived and was written too. Both attempts share one operation id, so a key for preventing this already exists; it is not enforced at the point of writing. A prompt change cannot fix this. It is Marta's item 1, and it is the same mechanism that drove the cost spike.

The record claim shows the confidence score is not a safety signal. The source says Mensah tied the Waves' franchise record and that the league record is 17. The card says he set a new league record and broke it. Two errors, 0.94 confidence. The correct coach statement scored 0.89. The ranking logic uses this number. The revised card format replaces it with a list of claims for the approver to check, and ranking falls back to recency and category priority. That is a product change, so it is Lewis's decision 4.

The two silent losses are the most dangerous. The Cole injury update failed validation and was never retried. The tipoff change was classified "routine" and dropped. Neither reached the queue, so Sam could not have seen either, and there is no routine comparison of sources against the queue. An approver reviewing cards alone would have approved a clean-looking set while the two most useful items of the morning were missing. The reconciliation script in this pack is the first such comparison.

The cost spike was retries, not volume. Items rose 2 percent on 2 September. Retry calls rose from 63 to 2,412. Cost per call stayed flat at about EUR 0.018, so retries are billed in full. At least 804 of 1,390 operations retried at least once. Generation latency in the replay sat between 7 and 21 seconds against a 15-second timeout, so a modest slowdown at event volume, about five times a normal hour, would do the same again.

Governance. The pack states that a human must approve cards before the client sees them, and also that the client was sent a selection from a queue marked "before operator review". Someone chose that selection and no record says who or why. This is not an accusation; it is a gap the client update asks Priya to close by finding out which cards were sent.

Demonstration

The step I took as far as I could: make the evidence reproducible and the instructions testable outside production. Everything else in this brief rests on both.

# 1. Join sources, log and queue; write the fact table and findings
python3 -I checks/reconcile.py data outputs
# 2. Compute every number quoted in the documents
python3 -I checks/numbers.py data outputs
# 3. Check both against hand-counted expectations
python3 -I checks/test_reconcile.py
# 4. Run current and revised instructions over all ten items, live
python3 -I prompts/run_prompts.py data/01_source_content.json outputs/prompt_runs --model sonnet
#    or, with an API key:  --backend api --model claude-sonnet-5-5
# 5. Build the before-and-after comparison with an automatic claim check
python3 -I prompts/compare_runs.py data/01_source_content.json data/02_trial_recommendations.json outputs/prompt_runs outputs/prompt_before_after.md

Steps 1 to 3 need only Python. Step 4 needs either a logged-in Claude Code CLI or an Anthropic API key; each saved output records the exact model id and run time. The runs used here are committed in outputs/prompt_runs/ and the comparison in prompt_before_after.md, so the result can be read without running anything.

What it shows

  • The reconciliation derives the duplicate, the lost item, the rejected item, the latency pattern and the cost pattern from the raw files, quoting the rows. It is the source-to-queue comparison the pilot does not have.
  • The automatic claim check flags the two faulty pilot cards. It also flags "before Game 3" against "before Friday" on the promotion card, which a person overrules because Game 3 is on Friday. That is what the check is for: narrowing what a person must read, not replacing them.
  • The revised instructions produce a correct Mensah card, reject the sponsor competition as promotional, classify the tipoff change as a high-priority schedule change, keep "questionable" and "sources say", and return a list of claims for the approver instead of a confidence score. For Mensah the list reads "the record is tied, not broken" and "franchise record, not a league record".

What it does not show

The current instructions, on today's model, also get many things right, and they vary between runs: in one run the current relevance step accepted the Mensah record item, in another it rejected it as not relevant. So the pilot's errors are partly model and pipeline behaviour, and this test proves the revised instructions are better specified, not that production will behave the same. Only Marta's rerun through the deployed path proves that, which is why it is acceptance criterion 2. The revised instructions also have rough edges in the saved runs: one card calls the Waves "opponent" because the highlights source never names them, which is the rule working on a thin source; one coach statement came back high when the rule says medium, which the checklist's priority step catches.

What still needs human judgment

Whether a highlights video is a second card. Whether promotions ever belong in the feed. How an unconfirmed report should look. Every approval. The story key the revised instruction emits is a hint for the approver and nothing more: the two Game 2 cards received different keys, and the official Cole update and the CourtsideWire report received the same one. Deduplication stays with engineering, on source and operation IDs.

How I used AI and how I checked it

Full chronological log, with the representative prompt and output, in ai_log/ai_use_log.md.

My tool of choice was Claude Code. It read the pack alongside me, wrote the scripts and tests, drafted the revised instructions, and produced the first draft of every document here. I read the pack myself, set the questions, and required it to prove each finding from the data rather than from its reading. I had every number computed by a script because language models misread numbers. I had the pack scanned for hidden text. I commissioned two separate AI review sessions with no access to our conclusions: a cold adversarial read of the pack, then a simulated hiring panel grading the finished draft. I decided what to accept, change and reject from each. The recommendation, the scope, the second-approver ask and the CourtsideWire position are my decisions.

Checked

  • Every finding cites a row in the files. Tests encode hand counts written before the script ran. Every figure in prose comes from the numbers script.
  • The cold read agreed with the first eight findings and added eleven, including that Sam leaves before tipoff and that Marta's window opens before Lewis decides.
  • The panel found a remit violation in the client update, an impossible Monday gate, unobservable stop conditions, two factual errors and eight contradictions between documents. All are fixed in this version and listed in the log.
  • A scan of the pack for hidden characters, direction overrides, embedded markup and unexpected fields. Clean.

Changed

  • The client update, rewritten so it precedes Lewis's decision rather than pre-empting it.
  • The Monday gate, rebuilt around Friday's rerun and Monday's session after the first version depended on Tuesday's data.
  • Stop conditions, rebuilt around what can be observed on the night.
  • The revised instruction's time-zone rule, moved out of headlines after it produced "(zone not stated)" in a title.
  • The prompt runner, changed to record the exact model id and time of each run and to accept an API key as well as the CLI.

Rejected

  • The AI's first framing of the duplicate as a prompt problem. The log shows a retry bug.
  • A hypothesis from the first review that the client's complaint referred to earlier output. The pack says the client was sent a selection from this trial. I had accepted it; the panel caught it.
  • The AI's capacity model of 50 cards an hour, in favour of Sam's observed 30 to 40.
  • The panel's view that a ten-minute dwell stop condition is too loose. Kept, because the target is a request rather than a measured service level, and a stop that fires on one slow card would pause the feed constantly.

Limitations

The prompt test proves instructions, not production. Outputs vary between runs, which is why each saved run records its model id and time. The story key is not a deduplication key. The AI wrote 8x where the figure is 5x and 45 percent where it is 44, and listed a pack fact as an assumption; the numbers script and the panel caught all three. The AI could not name the production model, see the 2 September queue, or measure the slow-review share. Those are labelled as assumptions or open questions throughout.
