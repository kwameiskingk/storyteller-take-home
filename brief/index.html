The situation

What the service is, where the pilot stands, and who has asked for what.

Storyteller is piloting a content opportunity service for the Aurora Basketball League's digital editors. Approved feeds from the league, the teams and a broadcast partner are polled every five minutes. An AI step decides whether each item is relevant; a second step writes a recommendation card with a headline, a summary, a priority and source links. An operator, Sam, approves cards before the client sees them. Two client editors then decide what to publish on the league's app and website. The service does not publish anything itself.

The Finals are tied 1-1. Game 3 is on Friday 11 September at 19:00 UTC, eight days from now. The client has planned editor training for Wednesday 9 September. This morning Jordan Reed, the client's Director of Digital Content, reported that a Game 2 result card appeared more than once in a sample they were sent, and that a card said Tayo Mensah had set a league record when he had tied a Waves record. Jordan asked whether they can still count on the service for the Finals, and whether CourtsideWire, an external outlet that was ahead of the official team account on an injury story, could be added.

The pack numbers the ten source items in the morning's replay src_001 to src_010 and the eight cards it produced rec_001 to rec_008. Those labels are used below.

Why not full scope

In the ten-item replay the pilot produced a duplicate card from a retry fault, fabricated a record claim at 0.94 confidence, lost the player availability update without telling anyone, rejected the tipoff change as routine, and ranked a sponsor competition as high-priority player news. That is five faults in nine approved items. Forecast volume is 240 cards in six hours with peaks of 80 an hour. Sam reviews about 30 to 40 cards an hour and leaves thirty minutes before tipoff. A full launch would put wrong or missing information in front of the editors during the Finals, at a volume nobody can review.

Why not withdraw

The causes are specific and each has an owner. The duplicate and the lost item are bounded engineering changes that Marta can size for Friday. The record claim, the rejected schedule change and the promotion are instruction and process changes, drafted and tested outside production. The client keeps its safety net. Withdrawing from a fixable problem gives up the one event Product wants to prove the service on, and tells the client we could not recover from two faults in a week.

CourtsideWire

The client is right that CourtsideWire was ahead: 22 minutes before the Waves' official medical update. The report also rested on "two people described as being close to the team" and said Cole was "expected to miss Game 3", where the team said "questionable, game-time decision". One item cannot show whether a source is reliable, and these two claims differ. Adding the source needs an ingestion change from Marta, a change to what Priya has told the client, and a product decision from Lewis on how an unconfirmed report should look. None of that fits in eight days alongside the fixes above.

After Game 3 I would run CourtsideWire in the background for four weeks, invisible to the client, and record two things for every story: how many minutes ahead of an official source it was, and whether an official source later confirmed it, contradicted it, or never addressed it. If the lead is real and contradictions are rare, I would propose a labelled unconfirmed-report lane, designed with Lewis.

Volume comes down in three layers before it reaches a person. Marta's fix removes duplicates at source: in the trial, one item in nine produced two cards. The revised relevance instruction rejects sponsor promotions and marks them so the approver can see they were seen. The two-lane split then keeps only results, availability, schedule and venue changes and official records live during the game. Four of the nine approved sample items are live-lane types. Applied to the forecast peak, about 36 cards an hour reach the approver.

Sam's stated rate is 30 to 40 cards an hour, which is 90 to 120 seconds a card. Thirty-six cards therefore need 53 to 71 minutes of a 60-minute hour. One approver is at the limit at best and over it at worst. If the highlights video were live as well, the share becomes five of nine and the peak needs 67 to 89 minutes; add routine fan information and it is 80 to 107. That is why both are held. Single-approver mode, results, availability and schedule or venue changes only, is three of nine: about 27 cards and 40 to 53 minutes.

These shares come from a ten-item sample that the pack says is not representative, and Sam's rate is described as indicative. Monday's logged session replaces both with measured figures before the client is told anything. Three consequences follow now.

  1. A second approver from 18:30 UTC is an explicit ask of Lewis and Priya. Sam's agreed cover ends at 18:30; tipoff is 19:00. Without a second approver, from tipoff through the result and the post-game peak the feed depends on one person who is also reconciling exports and handling escalations, so single-approver mode narrows what is live.
  2. Queue dwell is a stop condition rather than an aspiration.
  3. If Monday's measured live-lane share is well above 44 percent and no second approver exists, the recommendation becomes withdraw.

The ten-minute target

The client wants developments surfaced within about ten minutes of appearing in an approved source. The feed polls every five minutes, so up to five minutes are gone before anything happens. Generation took 7 to 21 seconds in the replay; the one timeout-and-retry path took 26 seconds, and 36 seconds is the worst case from the observed figures. A straightforward review is about a minute. That leaves roughly three and a half minutes for a card to wait in the queue. Achievable for one approver who does nothing else. Harder for one person who is also reconciling and escalating through the result peak, which is the single-approver case.

What the findings mean

The duplicate is a processing fault. The request for the Game 2 recap was declared timed out at 15 seconds and a retry started one second later. Six seconds after that the original response arrived and was written to the queue. Six seconds after that the retry arrived and was written too. Both attempts share one operation id, so a key for preventing this already exists; it is not enforced at the point of writing. A prompt change cannot fix this. It is Marta's item 1, and it is the same mechanism that drove the cost spike. One caveat: whether this is what the client saw depends on which cards they received. If they saw the recap and the highlights video, the repetition they noticed is editorial, and Marta has said she has not established the link. The client update asks the question that settles it.

The record claim shows the confidence score is not a safety signal. The source says Mensah tied the Waves' franchise record and that the league record is 17. The card says he set a new league record and broke it. Two errors, 0.94 confidence. The correct coach statement scored 0.89. The ranking logic uses this number. The revised card format replaces it with a list of claims for the approver to check, and ranking falls back to recency and category priority. That changes the card schema and the ranking, so it is a product and engineering change and sits with Lewis as decision 4.

The two silent losses are the most dangerous. The Cole injury update failed validation and was never retried. The tipoff change was classified "routine" and dropped. Neither reached the queue, so Sam could not have seen either, and there is no routine comparison of sources against the queue. An approver reviewing cards alone would have approved a clean-looking set while the two most useful items of the morning were missing. The reconciliation in this pack is the first such comparison.

The promotion card got three things wrong at once. It was ranked high as player news. It dropped the condition that entrants must request an insurance quote. And it changed "before Friday" to "before Game 3": the item was published on Thursday 3 September, so the deadline moved by a week. The automatic claim check caught the third one; I first overruled it as a fair paraphrase, and that overrule was wrong.

The cost spike was retries. Items rose 2 percent on 2 September. Retry calls rose from 63 to 2,412. Cost per call stayed flat at about EUR 0.018, so retries are billed in full. At least 804 of 1,390 operations retried at least once. Generation latency in the replay sat between 7 and 21 seconds against a 15-second timeout; normal days show 4 to 5 percent retries, so timeouts are intermittent, as Marta said, but the margin is thin at event volume, about five times a normal hour.

What the client was sent. The pack README says the client was sent a selection from a queue marked "before operator review". The capacity document says only approved cards have been shared. Both can be true if someone approved a selection by hand without logging it, which fits Sam's message that decisions have not been recorded. Either way there is no record of which cards went or who chose them. The client update asks Priya to find out.

Demonstration

The step I took as far as I could: make the evidence reproducible and the instructions testable outside production. Everything else in this brief rests on both.

# 1. Join sources, log and queue; write the fact table and findings
python3 -I checks/reconcile.py data outputs
# 2. Compute every number quoted in the documents
python3 -I checks/numbers.py data outputs
# 3. Check both against expected values read from the files
python3 -I checks/test_reconcile.py
# 4. Generic event check: duplicates, lost items, dwell, on any export in the pilot schema
python3 -I checks/event_checks.py data/01_source_content.json data/06_trial_run_log.csv data/02_trial_recommendations.json
# 5. Run current and revised instructions over the ten items, and over my four test cases
python3 -I prompts/run_prompts.py data/01_source_content.json outputs/prompt_runs --model sonnet
python3 -I prompts/run_prompts.py data_extra/candidate_test_cases.json outputs/prompt_runs_candidate_cases --model sonnet
#    or, with an API key:  --backend api --model claude-sonnet-5-5
# 6. Build the before-and-after comparison with an automatic claim check
python3 -I prompts/compare_runs.py data/01_source_content.json data/02_trial_recommendations.json outputs/prompt_runs outputs/prompt_before_after.md

Steps 1 to 4 need only Python. Step 5 needs either a logged-in Claude Code CLI or an Anthropic API key. Every saved run records the exact model id and time, and pins the scenario clock in the message so timeliness is judged against 3 September 2026 rather than the date the test happens to run. The runs used here are committed in outputs/, so the result can be read without running anything.

What it shows

  • The reconciliation derives the duplicate, the lost item, the rejected item, the latency pattern and the cost pattern from the raw files, quoting the rows. Its findings text is written for this pack. The generic event check has no prose: it flags duplicates by source and by operation, lost items, and dwell, and exits non-zero when anything is flagged, so it can gate a 15-minute check on the night.
  • The automatic claim check flags both faulty pilot cards, including the "before Friday" change I had wrongly waved through.
  • With the scenario clock pinned, the current instructions still rejected the tipoff change as routine, exactly as the pilot did, and gave the Mensah record low priority. On my own test cases they dropped a closed-entrance notice as "operational", the same class of failure. The revised instructions produced a correct Mensah card, rejected the sponsor competition, made the tipoff change and the entrance closure high-priority changes, kept "questionable", "ruled out" and "sources say", put the highlights video and the coach statement in the held lane, and returned a list of claims for the approver instead of a confidence score. For Mensah the list reads "the record is tied, not broken" and "franchise record, not a league record". A genuine league record in my test cases was reported as one, so the wording rule does not suppress real records.

What it does not show

The current instructions on today's model also get many things right, including Mensah's wording. So the pilot's errors are partly model and pipeline behaviour, and this test shows the revised instructions are better specified; it does not show that production will behave the same. Only Marta's rerun through the deployed path shows that, which is why it is acceptance criterion 2. Outputs also vary between runs, which is why each saved run records its model id and time, and why earlier runs are kept: a first set of revised runs before model ids were recorded, and a second set of both variants before the scenario clock was pinned, in which the model judged timeliness against November 2026.

What still needs human judgment

Whether a highlights video is ever a separate card. Whether promotions belong in the feed. How an unconfirmed report should look. Every approval. The story key the revised instruction emits is a hint for the approver: the two Game 2 cards received different keys, and the official Cole update and the CourtsideWire report received the same one. Deduplication stays with engineering, on source and operation IDs.

How I used AI and how I checked it

Full chronological log, with the representative prompt and output, in ai_log/ai_use_log.md.

My tool of choice was Claude Code. It read the pack alongside me, wrote the scripts and tests, drafted the revised instructions, and produced the first draft of every document here. I read the pack myself, set the questions, and required each finding to be proven from the data rather than from the AI's reading. I had every number computed by a script because language models misread numbers. I had the pack scanned for hidden text. I commissioned three rounds of separate AI review sessions with no access to our conclusions: a cold adversarial read of the pack, a simulated hiring panel on the first full draft, and a second adversary and panel on the finished version. I decided what to accept, change and reject from each. The recommendation, the lanes, the single-approver mode and the CourtsideWire position are my decisions.

Limitations

The prompt test proves instructions, not production. Outputs vary between runs. The story key is not a deduplication key. The AI wrote 8x where the figure is 5x and 45 percent where it is 44, listed a pack fact as an assumption, overruled a correct automatic flag, built a harness that leaked the real date, and left a placeholder in an earlier draft of the log. Each was caught by a script or a review round, which is the argument for running them. The AI could not name the production model, see the 2 September queue, or measure Sam's rate with the checklist. Those are labelled as assumptions or open questions throughout.
